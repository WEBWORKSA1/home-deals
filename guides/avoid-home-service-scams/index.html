Home repair scams and high-pressure sales: how to say no

The common patterns, your cancellation rights for sales made at your door, and what to do if something has already gone wrong.

Safety, 5 minute read

In short

  • A price that expires today is a sales tactic.
  • Most sales of $25 or more made at your home can be cancelled within three business days.
  • Never pay in full up front, and never sign over an insurance claim under pressure.

Most contractors are honest. The dishonest ones are easy to recognise, because they use the same handful of approaches and they all depend on speed. Slowing the conversation down defeats nearly every one.

The patterns

  • The storm chaser. Arrives after hail or wind, offers a free inspection, finds damage and asks you to sign immediately.
  • Leftover materials. A crew “just finished a job nearby” and can do your driveway or roof cheaply today, for cash.
  • The today-only price. A large list price, a dramatic discount and a call to a manager. Common with windows, solar, siding and water treatment.
  • The big deposit. Most or all of the money up front, followed by delays or disappearance.
  • The claim handler. Offers to “deal with the insurance” and asks you to sign over your benefits.
  • The free energy audit. A sales visit presented as a utility or government program.

Your right to cancel

The Federal Trade Commission’s Cooling-Off Rule gives you three business days to cancel most sales of $25 or more made at your home, and sales of $130 or more made at temporary locations such as hotel rooms and fairs. The seller must tell you about the right and give you two copies of a cancellation form. Many states add their own protections for home improvement contracts.

Five habits that protect you

  1. Never sign on the first visit. A real price is still there next week.
  2. Get three written quotes for the same scope.
  3. Verify the licence and insurance yourself, using the state board, not a number the salesperson provides.
  4. Pay by card or cheque, in stages, with a small deposit.
  5. Call your insurer yourself before agreeing to any insurance-funded repair.

If it has already happened

Cancel in writing within the cooling-off period and keep proof of delivery. Dispute card payments quickly. Report the business to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general, and to your local building department if unlicensed work was done.
